# Prior-Art Notes

This file holds historical references for Phase 3 prior-art passes. Notes are organized by source family. Each note names the mechanism, gives a sentence or two of evocative detail, and lists the pantry entry it sharpens when there is a direct pantry connection. References without a current pantry entry stay here as source material for future shelf passes.

The pantry stays the working surface. This file is the lab notebook for prior art.

## Clockwork: rack-and-snail, mutilated gears, Geneva stop

**Edward Barlow's rack-and-snail strike (1676).** Replaced the earlier count-wheel strike on English bracket clocks. The snail is a spiral-stepped cam attached to the hour-wheel pipe; at each hour, a rack-tail drops onto the snail's edge, and the depth of its drop tells the strike train how many teeth to count off before the bell. The cam is literally the schedule. The moment when the rack-tail falls off the snail's twelve-o'clock cliff back to one is called the *warning*. Sharpened **Snail cam dinner dwell**.

**Wilhelm Schickard's calculating clock (1623).** Introduced the *mutilated gear* — a wheel with only one tooth-set — to carry tens between digit columns in an adding machine. Pascal replaced this in the **Pascaline (1642)** with the *sautoir*, a weighted lever cocked by the previous digit's rotation and gravity-dropped onto the next column. The sautoir is the tone-perfect ancestor of any mechanism where several quiet steps of arming end in one decisive throw — held for any future "snap" entry on Stored-Energy Releases. Sharpened **Segment gear hesitation**.

**Geneva stop (Swiss watchmaking, 17th–18th c.).** A four- or five-slot wheel with one closed (convex) slot that hard-stops the winding pinion to prevent over-winding of a mainspring. The same indexing geometry was independently re-discovered for **film projector intermittent transport in 1896** (Oskar Messter, Max Gliewe, Robert William Paul's Theatrograph) so each frame would be held still in the gate while the shutter opened. The **Antikythera mechanism (c. 100 BCE)** uses a related but distinct *pin-and-slot* device to model the Moon's elliptical motion — the deepest known ancestor of pin-into-slot indexing. Sharpened **Geneva recipe wheel**.

Brown's *507 Mechanical Movements*: cam-and-follower family clusters near movements 95–97 (snail and heart cams illustrated as generic irregular cams); mutilated gears at 130, 133, 223, 282; intermittent gear motions at 63–76, 88, 211, 235, 241, 364, 398; Geneva stop at 212–215.

## Trigger interfaces: lever frames, keys, registers, dials, coin slots

**Mechanical railway lever frames and interlocking.** Signal-box levers use sliding locking bars, tappets, and notches so a route lever can physically prevent conflicting points or signals from being pulled. The relevant pantry move is not the railway theme; it is the hard mechanical rule that one selected path blocks incompatible paths. Sharpened **Signal-box meal lever frame**.

**Cash-register keybanks.** Mechanical registers turned a pressed key into several simultaneous facts: the selected amount was displayed, other keys were constrained by the keybank, and a drawer or bell could fire only after the key had committed. The useful pattern is a public selected-state flag coupled to a launch interlock. Sharpened **Cash-register meal keys**.

**Rotary telephone dials.** A finger wheel stores a hand input in a spring, then releases it through a governed return so the mechanism outputs a counted pulse train rather than a continuous gesture. The useful pantry translation is a meal selector whose return motion does the counting. Sharpened **Rotary-phone pulse selector**.

**Coin validators and vending-machine slots.** Coin mechanisms sort by diameter, thickness, edge, mass, or magnetic behavior, then turn a valid falling object into permission for a vend. The kitchen version should keep the visible sorting and return the token for reset rather than pretending to be money. Sharpened **Coin-slot meal tokens**.

**Bitted keys, warded locks, and pin-tumbler logic.** A shaped key converts profile into blocker positions; the important move is that the object the user holds carries mechanical information. Sharpened **Bitted spoon key**, **Cookbook page-notch selector**, **Order-spike ticket selector**, and **Placemat hole mask**.

## Theatre rigging: polyspaston, paradiso, hemp houses

**Vitruvian polyspaston (*De Architectura* X.2, c. 30–15 BCE).** Greek for "many-pulley" — the Roman compound crane in which a rope was carried over a top sheave, down to a lower block, around its sheaves, and back up, multiplying lift so four winch-men could raise three thousand kilos. Plutarch's *Marcellus* records Archimedes drawing a laden ship across Syracuse harbor through the same principle; Hero of Alexandria's *Mechanica* describes a similar machine. The canonical literary anchor for block-and-tackle as spectacle of mechanical advantage.

**Brunelleschi's *paradiso* (San Felice in Piazza, Florence, 1430s).** A copper-vaulted heaven-machine for the Annunciation procession, in which angels were lowered through cloth clouds on visible rope rigging while fireworks burned overhead. Vasari records that the audience was charmed precisely *because* they could see the ropes. The earliest documented instance of pulley spectacle deliberately staged for the audience rather than hidden — the cultural ancestor of any pantry mechanism where the ropes are the show.

**Giacomo Torelli, Teatro Novissimo, Venice (1641, *La finta pazza*).** Pole-and-chariot sub-stage rigging: a central drum with ropes routed to several flat-trolleys, so an entire scene could be changed in full view at the turn of one drum. Torelli was a shipyard pulley engineer before he was a theatre machinist; he was nicknamed *il gran stregone*, the great sorcerer, for the choreographies he could pull off from a single hand. The hemp-house tradition that followed — sandbag-and-rope counterweight rigging in court theatres, persisting into early-twentieth-century playhouses until the **Vienna Burgtheater's 1888 counterweight system** made it obsolete — is the canonical reference for visible weight under visible rope. R. W. Boychuk's *Nobody Looks Up: The History of the Counterweight Rigging System, 1500–1925* is the standard survey.

Sharpened **Pulley multiplication theater**. These references also remain useful for any future earnest-pulley entry that wants visible rope work to carry real load instead of only staging a gag.

Brown's *507*: pulley cluster at movements 1–22; block-and-tackle at movement 14.

## Automata: pegged barrels, Jaquet-Droz, jacquemarts

**Pierre & Henri-Louis Jaquet-Droz, *The Writer / Draughtsman / Musician* (Neuchâtel, 1768–1774).** The Writer alone carries a stack of forty cams translating shaped edges into the pen-path of a small boy writing a sentence; still operational at the Musée d'Art et d'Histoire de Neuchâtel. The Draughtsman draws four images; the Musician plays five tunes on a small organ. The canonical reference for indexed cam-shafts as program memory.

**Jacques de Vaucanson — Flute Player (1738), Digesting Duck (1739).** The Flute Player's pedestal contained a programmed cam-drum driving leather-fingered flute fingering; the Duck's pedestal a similar drum for wing, neck, and the famous bowel sequence. **Henri Maillardet's Draughtsman-Writer (c. 1800)**, now at the Franklin Institute, Philadelphia, uses seventy-two brass cams to produce four drawings and three poems — among the largest mechanical "memories" ever built before electronics. **Antoine Favre (Geneva, 1796)** is the canonical first cylinder-and-comb music box, the consumer-scale descendant of the pegged barrel.

**Jacquemarts** — animated bell-strikers on cathedral clocks. The oldest survivor stands on the south tower of **Notre-Dame de Dijon (1383)**, installed by Philip the Bold. **Strasbourg Cathedral's 1843 clock** carries the noon Apostles procession and a crowing rooster. **Prague's Orloj (1410, Mikuláš of Kadaň with Jan Šindel)** carries the Walk of the Apostles in twin windows. The cultural ancestor of any character that emerges from a wall to announce a bell.

Sharpened **Camshaft of appetizers** and seeded the new **Astronomical-clock figure carousel**.

Brown's *507*: cam-and-follower cluster at movements 95–97, 117, 130, 138, 149–150, 165, 217, 272, 276.

## Linkages: bell cranks and the Victorian wall

**Victorian and Georgian servant-call bell systems (mid-1700s onwards, ubiquitous in nineteenth-century British and American houses of any size).** A pull-cord in a parlor drove a wire through plaster that turned right-angle corners on **bell-cranks** — L-shaped iron levers pivoted at fixed points — and finally tugged a coil-sprung bell on a numbered or labeled board in the kitchen passage. Each bell hung on a spring so it kept *trembling* for several seconds after striking, giving the servant time to read which room had rung. The bell-crank gets its name from this exact application: it is the L-shaped lever the Victorians used to bend a servant's bell-rope around the inside of a wall. The bell board itself was called the **annunciator** or **indicator board**, and is the literal historical ancestor of a kitchen wall that rings for a room.

Sharpened **Bell-crank relay bank** and seeded the new **Servant-bell indicator board**.

Adjacent and held: English **change-ringing** (eastern counties of England, c. 1600). Permutation-ringing of tower bells by hand, following named "methods" — 3 bells produce 6 changes, 5 bells 120, 12 bells 479,001,600 — and one of the earliest concrete applications of group theory (Sarah Hart's Gresham lecture, *The Mathematics of Bell Ringing*, is the readable survey). Considered as a sequencer mechanism but skipped this round: converting method-ringing into a wall device requires more machinery than the gong court has room for. Reachable if a future entry wants meal cadences that are patterned rather than counted.

## Centrifugal governors: from windmill to brass balls

**Thomas Mead, Patent No. 1628 (1787).** "Regulator for Wind and other Mills" — the flyball governor as a windmill speed regulator, a year before Watt's steam-engine application and its proximate inspiration. Two weighted balls rise on hinged arms as the spindle spins faster; the collar they lift trims the millstone gap. The original wooden, agricultural form of the device.

**James Watt's centrifugal governor (1788).** Watt and Matthew Boulton adapted Mead's windmill device for Boulton & Watt steam engines, throttling the steam valve via the lifted collar. The brass balls and brass collar became the iconic image of mechanical feedback, even though feedback theory wouldn't be formalized until Maxwell's 1868 *On Governors*.

**Christiaan Huygens (*De vi centrifuga*, written 1659, published in *Horologium Oscillatorium* 1673).** The mathematical ancestor: the conical pendulum, a weight swinging in a horizontal circle, with centrifugal force derived a decade before Newton's *Principia*.

Sharpened **Flyball governor gate**.

## Variable gear trains: Antikythera to the rose-engine lathe

**The Antikythera mechanism (c. 100 BCE).** Recovered 1901 off the islet of Antikythera, now in the National Archaeological Museum, Athens. From a single hand-crank, compound gear trains (with prime factors 7 and 17 shared across multiple wheels) produced the Metonic nineteen-year cycle, the Saros eighteen-year-and-eleven-day eclipse cycle, sun and moon positions, and five planetary periods on separate dials. The earliest known device that produced different timings on different outputs from one input.

**Holtzapffel & Co., London (founded 1794).** Ornamental-turning and rose-engine lathes whose feed-rates and pattern ratios were selected by inserting interchangeable brass change-wheels into the geared train between mandrel and traverse. Holtzapffel rose engine No. 1636 (1886) is the canonical surviving masterwork. About 2,557 lathes were built between 1795 and 1928; only around fifteen were rose engines.

Adjacent and held: **Sturmey-Archer three-speed internal hub gear (1902)** — the domestic-scale "selectable ratio" object, in case a future entry wants a bicycle-coded selector rather than a brass-lever one.

Sharpened **Meal-ratio gear train**.

Brown's *507*: differential / epicyclic / change-gear cluster at movements 57–62, 260, 264; movement 5 is an early epicyclic train.

## Spiral funnels and tick-counters: clepsydra to wishing well

**Steve Divnick, Spiral Wishing Well (US patent, Divnick International, Dayton OH, 1985).** A parabolic funnel guides a coin through dozens of slowing orbits to a central hole, the descent itself doing the work of attracting attention; first installed at Wright-Patterson AFB in 1985, now standing in airports, museums, and charity vestibules worldwide. The canonical modern incarnation of orbit-as-delay.

**Ctesibius of Alexandria, water-clock (c. 250 BCE).** A float-and-gear clepsydra: water flowed slowly into a reservoir, a float rose, and through a ratchet-and-pinion turned an hour dial. The earliest documented device to convert slow continuous motion into discrete counted output — the conceptual ancestor of any *slow input → tick-counted release* mechanism. Held by Vitruvius and Hero as the most accurate timepiece of antiquity, unsurpassed until Huygens's pendulum clock of 1656.

Sharpened **Funnel orbit to ratchet stepper**.

Brown's *507*: ratchet-and-pawl cluster at movements 49, 75, 76, 78–80, 82, 206, 225, 236, 271.

## Looms: Jacquard cards and programmed pattern

**Joseph-Marie Jacquard, attachment for the Vaucanson loom (Lyon, 1804).** Lifted the warp threads for each pick of the shuttle by reading a chain of punched cards: pins were pressed against the card, those above holes passed through and lifted the corresponding hooks, those over blank card were blocked. Built on **Basile Bouchon (1725, perforated paper roll)**, **Jean-Baptiste Falcon (1728, punched-card chain)**, and **Vaucanson's automated loom (1740)**. The conceptual ancestor of every device that uses a physical pattern as a routing program — including, eventually, Babbage's Analytical Engine and Hollerith's tabulating machines.

Seeded the new **Jacquard meal-card selector**.

## Held references and skipped candidates

References that surfaced during the prior-art pass but did not become pantry entries this round:

- **Mutoscope flipbook drum** (Herman Casler, 1894; American Mutoscope and Biograph Co.). Hand-cranked drum of about 850 photo cards on radial vanes; geared-down crank produces persistence-of-vision animation. Penny-arcade staple through mid-twentieth century. Considered but skipped because on this wall it would only narrate without doing mechanical work — too close to decoration. Reachable if a future entry wants vaudeville-coded narrative motion.
- **Carillon programmable peg drums** (Mechelen and other Low Countries belfries, sixteenth century onwards). User-reprogrammable bell sequencers via removable pegs in tapped holes. Already covered by the pantry's existing **Pegboard program drum** entry.
- **Pascal's *sautoir*** (Pascaline, 1642). The gravity-thrown carry lever. Held here as the tone-perfect ancestor of any future "snap" or "throw" mechanism on Stored-Energy Releases; partially in use already via **Segment gear hesitation**.
- **Differential gear** (Onésiphore Pecqueur, 1827; Richard Roberts, 1832). Three-shaft averaging device. Considered as a meal-vote combiner — a gear-based alternative to the **Mechanical AND gate** — but skipped this round because its visual density may fight the wall's silhouette. Held for the next pass if AND-style routing needs a smoother version.
- **English change-ringing** (see *Linkages* section above).
